Puppies, especially neonates and those who have not completed their vaccination series, are the most vulnerable population in the kennel. This plan is compiled from authoritative veterinary sources (the Merck Veterinary Manual, AKC, WSAVA, AVSAB, the Royal Canin Academy, Cornell/UF shelter medicine, and peer-reviewed literature). It is aimed at the 15 puppies in the FluffyWA puppy area (K4), with the goal of minimizing preventable deaths.

> **Important disclaimer**: The clinical thresholds for temperature, blood glucose, weight, and so on in this plan come from authoritative sources, but **any clinical treatment decision must be confirmed by the supervising veterinarian**. A puppy's condition can deteriorate sharply within hours—when in doubt, contact the veterinarian immediately.

## Why Puppies Die: Know the Enemy First

- Neonatal puppy mortality is approximately **12–36%**, and **about half of these deaths occur in the first week after birth**
- **In about 85% of puppies that die within the first month, symptoms appear less than 5 days before death**—deterioration is extremely rapid and the signs are subtle, which is why monitoring must be frequent
- The main causes of death fall into four categories: maternal, environmental, congenital defects, and infection. Among these, the "three big killers" that staff can directly control are **hypothermia, hypoglycemia, and dehydration**—they compound one another, forming the classic "fading spiral." Together with missed colostrum and neglected low body weight, these are all preventable

## 1. Thermoregulation: Puppies Cannot Regulate Their Own Temperature (The Most Deadly, and the Most Preventable)

Puppies **cannot regulate their own body temperature before about 4 weeks of age**, so the ambient temperature directly determines life or death.

**Ambient temperature (by age in days)**:

| Age | Ambient temperature |
| --- | --- |
| Days 1–4 | **29.4–32.2°C** |
| Days 7–10 | Gradually reduce to **about 26.7°C** |
| End of week 4 | **about 22.2°C** |

- Puppy **core body temperature**: 35–36°C in the first week (this counts as low by adult-dog standards but is normal for neonates); rising to 37–38°C at 2–3 weeks
- A **core body temperature below 35°C** leads to a drop in heart rate, loss of the sucking reflex, hypoglycemia, gut stasis, and multi-organ failure
- **Never feed a chilled puppy**: hypothermia causes the gut to shut down, so milk sits undigested → reflux and aspiration, which can be fatal. You must **first rewarm the puppy slowly over 30–60 minutes** (rewarming too fast causes dehydration), and only feed once the puppy feels warm to the touch and can suckle

**Key points for keeping puppies warm**:

- **The heat source must not touch the body directly**: have the heating pad/lamp cover only one side of the whelping box, leaving a cool retreat zone the dam and puppies can actively move to, to prevent overheating and burns; always lay a cloth over the pad so puppies never touch the heating surface directly
- **Judge temperature by huddling behavior**: puppies that **huddle in a pile, stack on top of the heat source, and cry continuously** = too cold; **spread apart, away from one another, and panting** = too hot; **sleeping loosely on their own** = the temperature is right
- **How to measure temperature**: use a digital rectal thermometer with lubricant, inserted gently, to measure core body temperature; feeling whether the ear tips, foot pads, and mouth are warm works as a quick first screen but cannot replace an actual reading
- **How to rewarm**: warm slowly by wrapping the puppy against the body plus a warm environment (incubator/heating pad); skin-to-skin contact with the dam can be provided at the same time. Reassess every 15 minutes during rewarming, and reassess the sucking reflex once the puppy is back up to temperature

> ⚠️ **Warning**: Damp cold is an invisible killer. A puppy that is not dried off after birth, or bedding soaked with urine and milk, will make a puppy lose heat rapidly—keeping bedding dry and changing it promptly is as important as keeping warm.

## 2. Colostrum and Feeding: Seize the Immunity Window, Prevent Hypoglycemia

**Colostrum (passive immunity)**:

- Only about 5–10% of a puppy's antibodies come from the placenta; **the vast majority come from colostrum**
- Antibody absorption **peaks about 4 hours after birth and essentially stops after 12–16 hours**—the conservative operating rule: **the first 12 hours are the most critical; make sure every puppy gets colostrum as early as possible**
- The immunity conferred by colostrum protects for about **10–12 weeks**; puppies that miss colostrum are at extremely high risk of infection/sepsis

**Feeding frequency and amount (orphaned/requiring hand-feeding)**:

- Use a **canine milk replacer** (such as Esbilac); **never use cow's milk** (the protein/fat/lactose ratios are wrong)
- About **30ml per 100g of body weight** per day, in divided feedings
- **Feed every 2–3 hours in the first week** (equivalent to natural nursing 8–10 times per day) to prevent hypoglycemia, then gradually extend to every 3–4 hours, then every 4–6 hours
- **Weaning**: begin introducing gruel from a shallow dish at 3–4 weeks, completing the transition to puppy food by 6–8 weeks

**Hand-feeding technique details**:

- **Posture**: feed the puppy **lying on its belly with its head slightly raised** (the posture in which it naturally suckles from the dam), **never on its back facing up**—feeding on the back very easily leads to milk entering the trachea and causing aspiration pneumonia
- **Milk temperature**: after mixing, warm the milk replacer to **about body temperature (37–38°C)**; a drop on the inside of your wrist should feel warm but not hot; mix fresh for each feeding, and do not refrigerate mixed milk for more than 24 hours
- **Teat**: size the opening so that when the bottle is inverted the milk **drips out slowly, one drop at a time**; an opening that is too large gives a fast flow that causes choking; gently squeeze the bottle to control the flow while feeding
- **Burp + stimulate elimination after feeding**: after each feeding, gently wipe the perineal area with a warm, damp cotton ball to mimic the dam's licking and stimulate urination and defecation (puppies cannot eliminate on their own until about 3 weeks of age)
- **Signs of overfeeding**: excessive abdominal distension after feeding, diarrhea, or milk coming back out of the nostrils indicate feeding too much too fast—reduce the amount and slow down

**Hypoglycemia control**: Neonatal puppies have almost no glycogen reserves; without feeding, their liver glycogen is depleted in about 24 hours (faster in sick or weak puppies). Signs: weakness, trembling, persistent crying, a cold body, and going limp. Reference blood glucose: normal is about 90–200 mg/dL; **on the first day, <92 mg/dL raises the risk of death, and <40 mg/dL is severe hypoglycemia** (defer to veterinary judgment). Prevention relies on **frequent feeding + warmth**.

> ⚠️ **On-the-spot first aid for weak pups / suspected hypoglycemia (act while calling the vet)**: ① Warm the puppy at the same time, and never force-feed a cold puppy; ② if the puppy **still has a swallowing reflex**, you can rub a small amount of glucose water or honey/corn syrup onto the gums/under the tongue (simply pouring in liquid carries an aspiration risk, so keep the amount small); ③ **never give sugar by mouth to a puppy that is already unconscious or has no swallowing reflex**—take it straight to the vet for intravenous glucose; ④ the three big killers (hypothermia, hypoglycemia, dehydration) compound one another into a "fading spiral," so they must all be corrected at once, not just one of them.

## 3. Weight Monitoring: The Most Sensitive Early Warning

A daily weight chart is the single best tool for catching problems early—weight decline often appears before other symptoms.

- Weigh once at 12 hours and once at 24 hours after birth, then **weigh at the same time every day** (using a gram scale), continuing for at least the first 2–3 weeks
- **Normal weight gain: about 5–10% of birth weight per day**; **birth weight should roughly double by days 7–10**
- **Red-flag signs (require intervention/contacting the vet)**: no weight gain, stalled weight, **any weight loss**; a **weight loss of more than about 4%** significantly raises the risk; a puppy that is weak, cold, not seeking the nipple, or crying persistently

**Weighing record sheet (one per litter, posted next to the whelping box)**:

| Puppy ID (collar color/mark) | Birth weight | Day 1 | Day 2 | Day 3 | … | Daily change | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| e.g. Red | ___ g | ___ g | ___ g | ___ g | | +/- % | Alertness/suckling |

- **Give each puppy a unique ID**: mark them with a non-toxic, safe colored collar thread or a dot of nail polish, to avoid mixing them up when weighing
- **Weigh under fixed conditions**: weigh **at the same time, on the same gram scale, before feeding** each day to reduce error
- **Compare within the litter**: the one that consistently lags at the bottom of the litter and is clearly lighter than its littermates (the "weak pup") should be watched closely—it is often the first to run into trouble

## 4. Parvovirus Control and Immunization: Crossing the "Window of Susceptibility"

The **window of susceptibility** is the core concept: maternal antibodies halve about every 10 days, dropping to a level that is no longer sufficient to protect yet can still interfere with the vaccine response—typically **8–12 weeks, with some puppies still vulnerable at 16 weeks**. This is why a **series** of vaccines is needed, and why puppies partway through their series still face real risk.

**Immunization schedule (DHPP core vaccine, AAHA + WSAVA)**:

- **Begin at 6–8 weeks**, then **one dose every 2–4 weeks** thereafter
- **The final dose at 16 weeks of age or later** (WSAVA 2024 recommends ≥16 weeks; in high-risk areas often 18–20 weeks)—only at ≥16 weeks do maternal antibodies reliably disappear
- **Key operating rule: a puppy is not truly protected until about 1–2 weeks after the final dose at ≥16 weeks**

**Deworming (CAPC/AAHA)**:

- **Begin at 2 weeks of age**, repeating at 4, 6, and 8 weeks (i.e., every 2 weeks until about 12 weeks), then monthly until 6 months of age
- Targets roundworms and hookworms (transmitted transplacentally and through milk); deworm even when asymptomatic; praziquantel/pyrantel pamoate is safe from 2 weeks of age (follow veterinary guidance)

**Isolation**: keep puppies that have not completed their vaccinations strictly separate from the adult population; parvovirus spreads via **fomites** (hands, shoes, clothing, equipment, floors). Cleaning order: **the most vulnerable puppies first, sick/exposed dogs last**; wash hands and change clothes between groups.

## 5. Sanitation: Take Extra Care with Chemicals Around Neonates

- **Avoid the fumes of bleach, phenolics (pine oil/Lysol-type products), and quaternary ammonium compounds around neonates**—the volatile gases can harm the respiratory tract and precipitate fading. For routine gentle cleaning, use **chlorhexidine**—but note that **chlorhexidine does not kill parvovirus**
- **When you need to kill parvovirus** (empty kennel, nursery turnover, after exposure): first clean thoroughly to remove organic matter, then use **bleach at 1:32 with a contact time of ≥10 minutes**, or **accelerated hydrogen peroxide at 1:16 for about 5 minutes** (gentler), or potassium peroxymonosulfate
- After disinfecting, **ventilate thoroughly, rinse clean, and air-dry** before returning puppies to the kennel; never let disinfectant fumes accumulate around puppies
- The puppy nursery is **completely separate from the adult/boarding population**: dedicated equipment and bedding, ideally dedicated staff and PPE

## 6. Socialization vs. Disease Risk: Don't Throw the Baby Out with the Bathwater

This is the trade-off most easily gotten wrong.

- The **golden window for socialization is roughly 3–14 weeks**; missing it causes lifelong fear/aggression problems
- **AVSAB's core position**: a dog's risk of dying from parvovirus/distemper is **far lower** than the risk of being euthanized for behavioral problems—**behavioral problems (not infectious disease) are the leading cause of death in dogs under 3 years old**. So socialization **should not** wait until the full vaccine series is complete
- **Safe balance rule**: puppies can begin structured socialization at **7–8 weeks**, provided they **have received at least one vaccine dose at least 7 days prior and have completed their first deworming**. A study of over 1,000 puppies found **zero parvovirus cases** in classes that met this minimum condition
- **Kennel practice**: socialize through **controlled, clean, disease-managed contact**—only with dogs known to be healthy/vaccinated, and on disinfected floors; avoid dog parks and high-traffic public ground until the full vaccine series is complete. **Do not fully isolate for the sake of "safety"**—that trades a small infection risk for a larger risk of behavioral harm

## 7. When You Must Seek Veterinary Care Immediately

Neonatal puppies **can deteriorate within hours**—"any neonate that is unwell is an emergency." Seek veterinary care immediately if any of the following occur:

- Not nursing, loss of the sucking reflex, weak and limp, or unresponsive
- No weight gain, stalling, or loss on the weight chart (especially >4%)
- A cold body, or being pushed out of the nest by the dam (a classic fading sign)
- Persistent crying (pain, hunger, hypoglycemia, or illness)
- Diarrhea (especially watery or bloody/with parasites) or vomiting (>2–3 times within two to three hours) → rapid dehydration
- Rapid, labored breathing, nasal discharge, coughing, pale/bluish gums (respiratory distress or aspiration)
- Trembling, twitching, or seizures (hypoglycemia/hypothermia)
- Signs of dehydration (tacky gums, slow skin snap-back, reduced urination)

**Overriding principle: puppies under 3 weeks of age are extremely fragile and deteriorate very fast—when in doubt, call immediately.**
