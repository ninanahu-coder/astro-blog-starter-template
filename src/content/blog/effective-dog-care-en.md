Caring for a dog well depends not on enthusiasm but on **method and consistency**. Across our four sites, FluffyWA cares for more than 160 adult dogs and 15 puppies every day. The following are the effective care practices we have developed in the field, and they apply equally to caring for a dog at home.

## 1. Feeding: Fixed Times, Fixed Portions, Fixed Diet

- **Fixed times**: Consistent mealtimes give dogs a sense of security and help their digestion settle into a routine. Our adult dogs get two meals a day (breakfast at 9:00, dinner at 15:15), and puppies get four meals (7:30, 9:00, 14:00, 15:15).
- **Fixed portions**: Portions are set by body weight and activity level—err on the side of less rather than more. Obesity is the most common health problem in boarding dogs, and every dog has its own feeding record sheet.
- **Fixed diet**: Any change of food must use the 7-day transition method (adjusting the mix of new and old food daily). A sudden change of food is the number one cause of diarrhea.
- **Watch the eating**: How fast a dog eats and whether it leaves food are the earliest health signals. We require staff to log each dog's food intake within 15 minutes of feeding.
- **Always have water available**: Fresh water is provided nonstop throughout the day, and water bowls are washed daily. Large dogs that gulp their food down use a slow-feeder bowl to reduce the risk of bloating and vomiting.
- **A calm feeding environment**: When multiple dogs share an area, space them out or stagger their feeding to avoid conflicts from food-stealing or food-guarding.

## 2. Exercise: Quality Matters More Than Duration

- **At least two rounds** of outdoor activity each day, 15–30 minutes each time. This suits a dog's energy rhythm better than a single two-hour session.
- **Separate large and small dogs** during activity, and stagger dogs whose temperaments clash into different groups—safety always comes before efficiency.
- Exercise is not just running: sniffing is a dog's most important way to relieve stress. Letting dogs sniff freely along the fence line in the activity yard is more effective than forcing them to run.
- Puppy exercise should be **short and frequent**, carried out in a separate area to avoid overloading the joints.
- **Adjust timing for Perth's hot weather**: In summer, move outdoor activity to the cooler early morning and evening hours, and only do short indoor or shaded activity during the hot midday. Scorching ground can burn paw pads (use the five-second test—press the back of your hand to the ground; if it's too hot for your hand, don't let dogs out).
- **Provide water and shade at all times**: The activity yard always has shade and fresh water on hand. Brachycephalic (flat-nosed) breeds (bulldogs, pugs, etc.), senior dogs, and overweight dogs are more prone to heatstroke, so their sessions must be kept especially short.

## 3. Health Monitoring: Three Checks a Day, Isolate at the First Sign

The core of effective care is **early detection**. Here is our approach:

| Checkpoint | Time | What to Look For |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Morning check | At opening | Alertness, eye and nose discharge, stool form |
| Midday check | Midday round | Water intake, any limping after exercise |
| Evening check | Before closing | Appetite, coat condition, steady breathing |

If you notice any one of loss of appetite, diarrhea, coughing, or persistent scratching, isolate the dog for observation immediately and record it; after two consecutive abnormal readings, contact the vet. **Isolation is not punishment—it protects the other dogs.**

**Heatstroke is the number one emergency in a Perth summer. Recognize the signs**: relentless heavy panting that won't stop, heavy drooling, red or dark gums, unsteady standing, vomiting or diarrhea, and lethargy. If you suspect heatstroke, immediately move the dog to a shaded area, wet its body with room-temperature (not ice) water, run a fan to cool it, provide water, and contact the vet at once—heatstroke progresses extremely fast and can be fatal within minutes. **Never leave a dog alone in a car or an unventilated kennel.**

## 4. Cleaning and Disinfection: The Environment Is Health

- Disinfect the kennels daily, wash food and water bowls daily, and change bedding often—the vast majority of skin conditions and intestinal infections come from the environment.
- Use a **puppy-safe disinfectant** in the puppy area, and ventilate thoroughly after disinfecting before letting dogs back in.
- When staff move between different groups of dogs, they must wash their hands and change or disinfect their footwear. This is the first line of defense in kennel disease control.

## 5. Emotions and Socialization: The Overlooked Half

Boarding dogs experience separation anxiety when away from their owners. Effective ways to comfort them:

- **Keep familiar scents**: Allow owners to leave an old piece of clothing or a blanket the dog regularly uses.
- **Reduce stimulation for the first 48 hours**: Place newly arrived dogs in a quiet kennel first, and don't rush them into group activities.
- **A consistent carer**: Wherever possible, have the same staff member look after the same group of dogs—dogs recognize people faster than places.
- **Socialize gradually**: First let them sniff each other through a fence, then share an activity space. Forcing socialization creates fights and lasting fear.
- Increased barking, refusing food, and frequent paw-licking are all signs of stress. The response is more companionship and exercise, not scolding.

## 6. Special Rules for Puppies

1. Puppies that have not completed their vaccinations **must never have contact** with outside dogs.
2. Feed small, frequent meals of softened puppy food, and let them rest for half an hour after eating before any activity.
3. A fixed socialization period each day: exposure to different people, sounds, and floor surfaces—3 to 14 weeks is the golden window.
4. Record weight weekly, and report any abnormal growth curve immediately.

## 7. Daily Grooming: Coat, Nails, Ears, and Teeth

Cleaning and disinfection take care of the environment; daily grooming takes care of the dog itself—both are essential:

- **Brushing**: Brush long-haired dogs daily to prevent matting, and more often during shedding season. Brushing is also the best time to spot skin problems, parasites, and lumps.
- **Nails**: Check regularly. Overly long nails affect walking and split easily; if you hear a "click-click" as the dog walks, it's time for a trim.
- **Ears**: Drop-eared breeds (cockers, beagles, etc.) tend to trap moisture and become inflamed. Check regularly for any odor, redness, or discharge.
- **Teeth**: Provide chew toys or dental products. Worsening bad breath, drooling, or refusing hard food may signal an oral problem.
- **Bathe in moderation**: Bathing too often destroys the skin's oil barrier—bathe only as needed. Dry the dog thoroughly afterward, as dampness easily leads to skin conditions.

## 8. Same-Day Checklist for Newly Arrived Dogs

The day a new dog arrives is critical for establishing a baseline of health and trust. Check off each item:

- [ ] Verify owner information, emergency contact, and vet contact details
- [ ] Record vaccination and deworming history (flag any dog with incomplete immunization and manage under strict isolation)
- [ ] Record baseline condition: weight, alertness, coat, and any limping/coughing/diarrhea
- [ ] Take in familiar-scent items left by the owner, such as old clothing or a blanket
- [ ] Record feeding habits: food type, number of meals, portion size, dietary restrictions, allergies
- [ ] Record medications, chronic conditions, and behavioral traits (food-guarding, fear of thunder, separation anxiety, etc.)
- [ ] Assign a quiet kennel and reduce stimulation for the first 48 hours
- [ ] Set up the daily care record sheet

## 9. Records: Good Care Is Written Down

We require a daily record for every dog: how much it ate, how its bowel movements were, its performance during exercise, and its emotional state. The value of records lies in **trends**—a single day of poor appetite may just be the heat, but eating little for three days in a row is a problem. When an owner collects their dog, a complete care record is also the best proof of trust.

---

There are no shortcuts in dog care: feeding at fixed times and portions, plenty of exercise, three health checks a day, a clean environment, patient emotional support, and complete records. Doing every small thing well, again and again, is the most effective care there is.
