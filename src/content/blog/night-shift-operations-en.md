Day-shift scheduling ends at 18:00, but dogs — especially puppies and sick dogs — do not stop needing care overnight. This plan establishes **18:00–07:00 night operations**, built around holding three bottom lines with limited staff: **puppy survival, sick-dog safety, and emergency response**. Because staffing is tightest at night, coverage is tiered by risk — not every site needs someone on-site all night, but the K4 puppy area and isolation areas must be prioritised.

> Puppies — especially newborn and pre-weaning pups — still need feeding and warmth overnight; hypothermia and hypoglycaemia are the two biggest night-time killers of puppies. Sick dogs frequently deteriorate at night. These two facts mean we cannot simply "lock up and leave" overnight.

## 1. Night Coverage Model (Tiered by Site Risk)

| Site | On-Site Population | Night Deployment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **K4** | 10 adults + **15 puppies** | **1 attendant on-site all night** (focus: puppy feeding and warmth) |
| **K3** | 100 adults | Night patrol + remote monitoring + on-call nearby |
| **K2 / K1** | 35 / 15 adults | Remote monitoring + emergency on-call |
| Isolation area (any site) | Sick dogs | When sick dogs are present, add a night patrol at that site |

**Deployment principle**: Because of the puppies, K4 is the only site requiring someone on-site all night. Other adult sites are covered by an **evening final check + scheduled night patrols + remote monitoring + duty manager on call**. Any site that has an isolated sick dog must have a patrol scheduled that night.

> **Set the deployment each evening based on that night's actual situation**: before day shift closes up, the duty manager confirms — based on the number of puppies on-site that day, whether there are any newly sick/isolated dogs, whether any dam is close to whelping, and the weather (hot nights) — whether K3/K2/K1 need an added on-site patrol that night. The default minimum configuration is K4 on-site + the rest on remote monitoring, but whenever there is an isolated dog or a dam near whelping, the corresponding site must have someone on-site to patrol that night and cannot rely on monitoring alone.

## 2. Night Schedule

> **10 minutes of prep before starting the shift**: on arrival, take a face-to-face handover from day shift first — clarify which puppies and isolated dogs need close watching tonight, any daytime abnormalities and medications, and the status of dams near whelping; confirm your phone/radio is fully charged, the emergency contact list is in hand, the torch and backup heat source are ready, and the puppy feed is prepared. Only let day shift leave once the handover is complete.

### K4 Puppy Area (On-Site All Night)

| Time | Tasks |
| --- | --- |
| 18:00 – 18:30 | Take day-shift handover, count puppies, verify feeding/medication records |
| 19:00 | Puppy feeding (newborns every 2–3 hours, weaning pups per meal plan), check heating equipment |
| 21:00 – 22:00 | Full-area patrol: puppy temperature/huddling, adult condition; record |
| 22:00 – 23:00 | Puppy feeding (frequency by age in months), weigh any abnormal puppies |
| 01:00 – 02:00 | Night patrol + puppy feeding (newborns); check ambient temperature |
| 04:00 – 05:00 | Patrol + puppy feeding; log any overnight anomalies |
| 06:30 – 07:00 | Puppy early feed, hand over overnight status to morning shift |

> The 2–3 hour feeding rhythm for newborn/pre-weaning puppies must not be interrupted overnight. Before each feed, confirm the puppy is warm (rewarm a chilled puppy before feeding — never feed a cold puppy).

**The three puppy night checks** (run through quickly on each patrol; when in doubt, call):

- **Check warmth**: feel whether the puppy's ear tips, foot pads, and belly are warm; the whole litter huddling into a tight pile is often a sign the environment is too cold, while pups spread apart may mean it is too hot — cross-check the heating-zone temperature against age in days (29–32°C for the first 4 days, decreasing with age thereafter, judged by the pups lying relaxed and quiet without whimpering)
- **Check feeding**: whether they actively seek/take the teat, and whether any refuse to feed; refusal to feed + feeling cold is the earliest and most dangerous combination for hypoglycaemia/hypothermia
- **Check vitality**: whether they struggle vigorously when picked up and whether their cry is loud; a limp, cold pup with a faint cry must be rewarmed and reported immediately

> **Heating-equipment failure is the number-one overnight hazard**: on each patrol, take a moment to confirm the heat pad/lamp is powered, at the right temperature, and that puppies are not lying directly on an overheated surface; if the equipment fails, first maintain warmth with a backup heat source (a cloth-wrapped hot-water bottle, raising the room temperature) and then report it for repair — never let a puppy litter lose heat all night.

### Adult Sites (Patrol + Monitoring)

| Time | Tasks |
| --- | --- |
| 18:00 – 18:30 | Final check before day-shift lock-up at each site: top up water, check door locks and fencing, switch on night ventilation/climate control |
| 22:00 – 23:00 | Night patrol (on-site attendant or duty manager walking the site): quick check of each kennel, focus on isolated dogs |
| 02:00 – 03:00 | Remote monitoring review + on-site patrol if needed (mandatory on-site visit if isolated dogs are present) |
| 06:00 – 07:00 | Pre-opening morning patrol, hand over to morning shift |

## 3. Core Tasks That Must Be Done Overnight

1. **Puppy feeding and warmth** (K4): feed at the frequency for their age in months, confirm temperature each time; check heat pads/lamps, and ensure ambient temperature meets the standard for their age in days (29–32°C for the first 4 days)
2. **Sick/isolated dog patrols**: illness readily worsens at night; any site with isolated dogs must be patrolled on-site, and any abnormality handled per the emergency procedure; patrol isolated dogs **after the healthy dogs**, change gloves/disinfect your hands when entering and leaving the isolation area to avoid carrying pathogens to other dogs, and record feeding, drinking, elimination, breathing, and demeanour at each round to compare trends over time
3. **Security**: check all door locks, fencing, and gates; confirm no dogs have escaped and nothing is abnormal; log any intrusion/unusual noise
4. **Environment**: check that ventilation and climate control are running (hot Perth nights still require cooling/ventilation); check water supply
5. **Records**: fill in the night patrol log at each round, and confirm handover in person

## 4. Emergency Response (Most Critical at Night)

With few staff overnight, **a clear chain of response matters more than having more people**:

- **Tiered escalation**: frontline attendant spots an anomaly → immediately phone the duty manager → contact the vet if necessary
- **Emergency contact list** posted in each site's duty room: duty manager, on-call vet / 24-hour animal emergency, and each site lead's phone number
- **Puppy emergencies** (not nursing, cold and limp, seizures, diarrhoea/vomiting): puppies can deteriorate within hours — **when in doubt, call immediately**, first treating per the *Improving Puppy Survival Plan* (rewarm, prevent hypoglycaemia) before transporting to the vet
- **Adult emergencies** (bloat/gastric torsion, seizures, major bleeding, respiratory distress): immediately contact the duty manager and emergency vet; bloat in large, deep-chested dogs is an emergency where every second counts
- **Fire/safety**: the evacuation plan, fire extinguisher locations, and emergency dog relocation routes must be known by all staff

## 5. Remote Monitoring (Amplifying Limited Staff)

- **Cameras** cover each site's main kennel area, puppy area, isolation area, and entrances/exits; overnight the duty manager can remotely check multiple sites
- **Temperature/environment alarms**: install temperature alarms in puppy and isolation areas that automatically notify duty staff when thresholds are exceeded (especially to guard against heating-equipment failure causing puppies to lose body heat)
- **Access-control/intrusion alarms** integrated
- Monitoring is a **supplement, not a replacement** — puppy feeding and sick-dog treatment still require someone on-site

## 6. Staffing and Welfare

Night shifts are demanding on people, so scheduling must be sustainable:

- **Night-shift allowance**: pay night/weekend penalty rates (with the applicable loading) per the Animal Care Award — compliant, and key to retention
- **Fixed night-shift team + rotation**: avoid frequent day/night flips; ensure adequate rest after a night shift before returning to day shifts
- **Lone-worker safety**: a lone attendant on-site overnight must have a regular check-in mechanism (e.g. report in safe to the duty manager every 2 hours), carry a phone/radio
- **No heavy work on night shift**: night work focuses on care, patrols, and puppy feeding; save heavy tasks such as deep cleaning and deep disinfection for daytime hours when staffing is higher

## One-Page Night Checklist

- [ ] K4 staffed on-site all night; other sites get final check + patrol + remote monitoring + manager on call
- [ ] Any site with an isolated sick dog must be patrolled on-site that night
- [ ] Feed puppies overnight at the frequency for their age in months, confirm temperature each time, rewarm a chilled puppy first
- [ ] On each patrol, check door locks and fencing, ventilation/climate control, and water supply
- [ ] Emergency contact list (manager/emergency vet/site lead) posted in every duty room
- [ ] Lone workers report in safe to the manager every 2 hours
- [ ] Fill in the night patrol log at each round, hand over in person each morning
- [ ] Night-shift allowance paid in compliance, with adequate rest after night shifts

> This plan links the *Kennel Work Schedule* (daytime 07:00–18:00) with the *Improving Puppy Survival Plan* to form a 24-hour closed loop. Night staffing levels must be adjusted dynamically according to the actual number of puppies and sick dogs on-site.
